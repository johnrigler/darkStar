# Dark Star — John, Religious Privilege, and Secular Agency — 2026-10-03

This note develops the legal and institutional side of Ninth Baptist.

## Religious structure has material consequences

If Ninth Baptist becomes an actual institution, Dark Star should acknowledge that religion in the United States is not merely symbolic or private.

Religious status can interact with tax, property, employment, governance, housing, charitable organization, and other legal structures.

The parsonage / ministerial housing rules are a useful example.

The literary and political question is:

> Why should certain forms of collective life receive institutional benefits only when they are expressed through an accepted religious category?

This is a normative question, not a claim that current law already grants the same benefits to secular people.

## The parsonage example

Under current federal tax rules, qualifying ordained, commissioned, or licensed ministers may be able to exclude certain church-provided housing or a properly designated housing allowance from federal income tax, subject to statutory and regulatory limits.

That benefit is not a generic housing deduction available merely because a person creates a church-like organization.

Dark Star should therefore distinguish:

existing legal privilege

from

the argument that analogous secular forms of collective service should have access to comparable treatment.

## Use the structure lawfully

Ninth Baptist should not be framed as a sham device for pretending to be religious solely to obtain a tax benefit.

The stronger Dark Star idea is:

> If the law has created useful institutional machinery around religion, study what that machinery enables and ask why its useful functions should be confined to conventional religious storytelling.

If Ninth Baptist genuinely operates as a church or religious body under applicable law, it can make lawful use of the structures available to such institutions.

If it does not qualify, the project should not pretend otherwise.

The institutional experiment is more interesting when it survives contact with the actual rules.

## Storyteller in the Dark Star sense

Here, **storyteller** does not mean novelist, actor, preacher, or performer.

It means a person whose social legitimacy depends on sustaining an accepted account of who they are.

The concern is that legal and institutional benefits can sometimes attach to recognized categories:

minister
church
charity
professional
credentialed expert

while someone performing similar socially useful work outside the recognized category may receive fewer benefits.

The Dark Star question is:

> How much useful activity is shaped by the need to fit a recognized story about what kind of person or institution is doing it?

## Religion as a privileged interface

This connects to the other interface problems in Dark Star.

Law:
right -> procedure -> recognized legal actor

Public voice:
speech -> profile -> recognized speaker

Employment:
ability -> credential -> recognized worker

Religion:
collective practice -> recognized religious form -> institutional privilege

The issue is not that recognized categories have no purpose.

The issue is that the category can become the gateway through which practical agency must pass.

## Secular denominationalism

Ninth Baptist can push the idea of secular denominationalism further.

A group can ask what churches historically know how to do:

- house people;
- gather people;
- organize service;
- preserve memory;
- establish local trust;
- teach;
- create ritual;
- care for members;
- maintain intergenerational obligations;
- provide leadership roles;
- support people during transition.

Then it can ask which of those functions require supernatural belief and which are simply durable social technologies.

The recorso move is not to destroy the church form.

It is to recover the useful machinery inside it.

## Equality question

John can therefore pose a difficult question:

> If two communities perform comparable forms of care, service, education, mutual aid, and local governance, why should the legal treatment depend so heavily on whether one community can place those activities inside a recognized religious category?

Dark Star does not need to answer this conclusively.

The question itself connects law, religion, agency, and the age of stories.

## Benefits and burdens

Religious institutional status should not be treated as pure privilege.

It can also carry obligations, internal governance requirements, employment consequences, tax complexity, public scrutiny, and legal risk.

Likewise, clergy housing rules have limits.

The useful Dark Star argument is not:

religion gets free money.

It is:

> The state already recognizes some forms of collective life as institutionally special. That reveals that the legal system is capable of treating social organization as more than ordinary market activity.

The secular question is what other forms of useful collective life might deserve analogous recognition.

## John after Luke

This belongs in John because Luke has already driven the story economy to its extreme.

John is rebuilding institutions after the explosion.

The question is no longer:

> Which story wins?

It becomes:

> What structures actually let people live, care for one another, act, and carry agency forward?

Ninth Baptist is one possible answer.

Not because its religious label is magical.

Because the church form contains institutional tools worth examining, reusing, and, where lawful, fully exercising.

## Governing proposition

A useful John line is:

> I am not interested in pretending to be religious to obtain a benefit. I am interested in why useful forms of collective life must first become legible as religion before the law knows what to do with them.
