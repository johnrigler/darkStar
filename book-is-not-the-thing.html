The Dark Interval

Public space after the assumption of continuity.

John Rigler · October 2026

The old assumption was that the systems around us would remain basically continuous: governments would change but the order would persist, companies would fail but the network would remain, professions would evolve but the shape of work would survive, platforms would come and go but public communication would remain broadly available. That assumption is now harder to make.

1. A switch has flipped

The world is moving toward a more fragmented and multipolar order. Alliances are less stable. Trade and technology are increasingly shaped by national-security concerns. States are more willing to intervene in networks, information flows, platforms, payments, and infrastructure. At the same time, artificial intelligence is moving into white-collar work at a speed that makes old professional boundaries look unexpectedly temporary.

These are not separate stories.

They are arriving together.

The result is a peculiar historical interval: more power is concentrating in large technical and political systems at the same moment that individuals are acquiring tools that let them cross boundaries that once required institutions, departments, and specialist chains.

The center is becoming stronger and less trustworthy at the same time that the margin is becoming more capable.

2. Stasis can survive inside enormous change

White-collar systems often respond to uncertainty by becoming more procedural.

Responsibility divides into departments. Departments divide into roles. Roles acquire credentials, review gates, procurement paths, compliance layers, project-management systems, risk committees, approved vendors, and technical stacks.

Each layer may have a reason.

Together they can produce a strange form of stasis: the world changes faster while the organization becomes better at reproducing the structure it already knows.

uncertainty → more process → more specialization → more dependency → less ability to change direction

This is the institutional form of stack capture.

AI disturbs it because one person can now move across domains that previously required a relay race of specialists. The specialist still matters. The relay race is no longer sacred.

3. Public space cannot depend on permission from the center

A digital public square that exists only because one company permits it is not fully public.

A public archive that can be removed by one administrator is not fully public.

An identity that disappears when an account is closed is not fully yours.

A reputation that exists only inside one company's database is not portable.

A publication that survives only as long as its host, domain, platform policy, or payment provider allows it to remain visible is not durable enough for a period of political and institutional flux.

The risk is not merely technical failure.

Governments already block websites, restrict social platforms, shut down connectivity, compel removals, criminalize some online expression, and pressure intermediaries. Companies also moderate, rank, remove, suspend, deprecate, merge, and close systems according to their own rules and pressures.

The point is not that every government or platform is hostile.

The point is that a public medium should not require benevolence from every future government, platform owner, regulator, investor, or administrator through whose territory it passes.

Public space needs an exit from permission

A durable public medium should remain legible even when the preferred institution is no longer willing, able, or permitted to present it.

4. Multipolarity changes the threat model

A more multipolar world does not simply mean more countries matter.

It means more competing jurisdictions, standards, alliances, networks, payment systems, censorship regimes, identity systems, and claims of legitimacy can coexist.

The network no longer feels like one expanding universal space.

It begins to look like contested territory.

That makes portability more important.

An object that depends on one DNS name, one app store, one cloud provider, one national payment rail, one identity issuer, or one political jurisdiction carries more hidden fragility than it did when global integration seemed like a one-way process.

The answer is not to predict which center wins.

The useful question is how to build things that do not require a single center to win at all.

5. Saturnalia

Saturnalia is the old ritual of reversal.

The ruler and ruled exchange positions. Rank loosens. The respectable and disreputable trade masks. The center becomes unstable enough for the margin to become visible.

That is why Saturn belongs here.

Saturn is time, age, winter, harvest, limit, decay, memory, recurrence.

Saturn is also the old king afraid of succession.

The image is useful because mature systems often react to transition by tightening control over the channels through which replacement would occur.

platform ↔ artifact · account ↔ key · database ↔ ledger · center ↔ margin · consumer ↔ participant

Saturnalia does not predict history.

It gives a language for reversal when things that looked peripheral begin to look like survival tools.

6. Fourth Turnings, ricorso, and the return of old problems

The Fourth Turning describes history as recurring periods of institutional crisis and reorganization.

Vico described corso and ricorso: orders form, harden, decline, and return in altered form.

Neither needs to be treated as a discovered law.

Their value is that they take transition seriously.

They allow the possibility that institutions can reach a point where reform is no longer experienced as gradual improvement but as a reorganization of what counts as normal.

That possibility no longer feels remote.

Margin: Myth is not evidence. Cycles are not protocols. But maps become useful when the terrain stops behaving like yesterday.

7. Finnegans Wake belongs to a world that will not hold still

Finnegans Wake refuses to let language remain one thing at a time.

A word becomes a sound, a pun, a name, another language, a historical fragment, a joke, a river, a return.

Meaning survives by recombining.

That is close to the problem here.

A page may become a QR code. A QR code may become an address. An address may become a history. A history may become a reputation. A transaction may become a payment and a publication at once. A printed object may become source, index, artifact, instruction, or key to another medium.

The important property is not purity.

It is the ability to pass from one form to another without losing the thread.

8. Then AI enters the room

At exactly this point of geopolitical fragmentation and institutional fatigue, large language models appear.

They do not merely automate old tasks.

They lower the cost of moving among fields.

A person can move from code to cryptography to law to design to networking to writing to research without waiting for the old sequence of organizational handoffs to assemble itself around the problem.

This does not make expertise obsolete.

It makes free association operational.

That is historically awkward for institutions built around the scarcity of translation between specialties.

It is also dangerous, because the same AI systems can be centralized into a small number of extremely powerful platforms.

The emancipating tool and the concentrating tool are the same machine viewed from opposite ends.

AI increases the power of the individual and the power of the center at the same time.

9. Most reactions to crisis will reproduce the crisis

Periods of fear produce predictable responses.

More gatekeeping.

More official channels.

More identity checks.

More platform dependence.

More centralized databases.

More moderation by intermediaries.

More reliance on the institutions that already appear unable to adapt.

These reactions are understandable.

They may also deepen the dependency that made the system brittle.

The alternative is not disorder for its own sake.

It is to separate the durable object from the temporary institution around it.

10. A different public medium

A different digital public space can begin with a few simple inversions.

Identity

The person can hold the key before any service recognizes it.

Memory

The record can remain recoverable after the preferred interface disappears or is blocked.

Reputation

Evidence can accumulate across services instead of becoming one platform's property.

Exchange

Money can move through the same public medium without becoming the whole purpose of the medium.

Services remain.

Institutions remain.

Editors remain.

Moderators remain.

Courts remain.

Communities remain.

What changes is the assumption that any one of them must own the underlying identity, record, or coordinate in order to be useful.

11. Persuasion is the old model

Most political writing, religious writing, advertising, criticism, and literature still assume the same basic direction of travel.

Someone constructs a story.

The story attempts to change the reader.

The reader is expected to agree, convert, purchase, vote, believe, join, or behave differently.

author → message → audience → persuasion → action

That is not the model here.

The more interesting possibility is to give a very large number of people access to a new medium and let them discover uses that were not specified in advance.

The argument becomes explanatory rather than commanding.

It says: this is what you are looking at. This is why it behaves differently. This is why the old categories may be misleading.

But the important event has already begun before the explanation is complete.

A person can read an obviously human-readable address before understanding how the checksum was constructed.

They can follow a public coordinate before learning the vocabulary of self-sovereign identity.

They can enter a shared space, find another person, inspect a history, exchange value, add a record, or follow a thread before they have acquired a theory of what the system is.

Understanding does not have to come before participation.

This is a profound difference from a work whose success depends on convincing the audience that its interpretation is correct.

The medium succeeds when people can enter it, use it, misunderstand parts of it, invent around it, and still produce meaningful public artifacts.

12. Mogwai as a public square

Mogwai is the social surface of this idea.

It is difficult to call it social media without importing the assumptions of existing social-media platforms.

But it is a social medium.

It is a place where public identity, readable coordinates, durable records, media, reputation, and exchange can meet without requiring one company to own the underlying social graph.

The important difference is not visual.

A feed can still look like a feed. A profile can still look like a profile. A conversation can still look like a conversation.

The difference is underneath.

If the identity can survive the interface, if the record can survive the host, if another reader can reconstruct the public history, if another service can present the same coordinate, then the square is not identical to the company that currently renders it.

That makes possible a global public space with a different relationship between participant and platform.

People can arrive from different countries, communities, currencies, devices, and institutional systems without first agreeing on one central registrar.

They can carry context with them.

They can build reputation through accumulated public history.

They can exchange money when money is useful without reducing every relationship to a financial product.

They can publish, link, attest, play, argue, teach, sell, give, annotate, and invent new conventions.

The system does not need to know in advance which of those activities will matter most.

The square should be larger than its architect

If millions of people entered such a medium, the important result would not be that they all used it correctly. The important result would be that they could create public relationships and conventions that no original designer had to authorize in advance.

13. Recognition is not success

An idea can succeed inside the institutions of culture and still fail at its actual purpose.

It can become a paper.

It can become a respectable book.

It can become a conference talk, a podcast circuit, a course, a newsletter, a documentary, a branded community, or a successful channel.

All of those outcomes can look like victory.

They can also turn a new medium back into content.

An academically acceptable version could be cited, categorized, debated, and placed on the same growing heap of papers that describe systems nobody ever enters.

A commercially successful version could produce readers, customers, subscribers, followers, and an audience that remains structurally passive.

Neither is the relevant measure.

The measure is whether strangers can begin collaborating with one another in ways that do not depend on the original author remaining at the center.

The desired result is not an audience.

It is a population of participants.

People should be able to enter through different doors, recognize one another, publish, exchange, attest, organize, make tools, make games, create local conventions, build services, abandon services, fork interfaces, and teach the system to people the original designer will never meet.

This is grassroots collaboration in the literal architectural sense: coordination emerges from the edges because the shared medium is available there.

The scale matters.

A few technically sophisticated users can always assemble unusual tools for themselves. The more radical possibility is that a very large number of ordinary people can enter through readable artifacts and simple physical actions, quickly acquire enough orientation to participate, and then create the uses that make the medium socially real.

The essay is therefore not the achievement.

It is closer to a field guide left beside the entrance.

If all that remains is an elegant explanation, the project has missed its target.

The evidence of success would be people using the medium without needing the explanation anymore.

14. The essay comes after the event

The explanatory text should increasingly feel late.

Readable addresses, public histories, QR objects, alternate interfaces, local gatherings, new services, unexpected conventions, and relationships among participants should already be making the argument materially.

The text can explain what is happening.

It should not be the only thing happening.

15. Survival means replication

A durable medium cannot depend on one official copy.

It has to be copied.

It has to be forked.

It has to be printable.

It has to be reconstructable.

It has to tolerate unofficial readers, unofficial interfaces, unofficial gatherings, unofficial hosts, and unofficial continuations.

read → inspect → copy → act → modify → pass on

The goal is not immortality for one publication. The goal is enough replication that the idea can survive the death of its preferred container.

This is where a book stops being only a book.

A fixed printed spread carries the argument and the artifact.

A QR code can open a deeper online layer.

The online layer can expose transactions, source, tools, archives, and alternative routes.

If that route later disappears, the durable identifiers should still point toward reconstruction.

16. Post-collapse tools for a world that may not collapse

Keys, signatures, hashes, public ledgers, printed coordinates, and content-addressed files can sound like tools for catastrophe.

They do not require catastrophe.

They require only institutional mortality.

A government changes.

A company closes.

A platform becomes hostile to a class of speech.

A service becomes inaccessible in one country.

A professional system stops offering the path it once offered.

An archive is removed.

An account is lost.

A domain becomes useless.

The artifact should still make sense.

The person should still be the same person.

The history should still be recoverable.

The next reader should still be able to continue.

The dark star is not the end of the world

It is the moment when continuity stops being assumed, and survival becomes a design requirement.
